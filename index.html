01 / ORIGINS山と日本

A watch for a mountainous country

Seiko’s first sports watch was made for the people who walked into Japan’s mountains.

Seiko’s own account is short and specific. In 1959 it introduced the Laurel Alpinist as its first sports watch, for mountaineers and outdoor enthusiasts as climbing and trekking grew in popularity. Seiko lists the features: a screw-back case to keep out dust and sand, a leather pad between watch and wrist, and luminous hands and indices. Above all, the large mountain-peak markers at 3, 6, 9 and 12 stood for the four compass points.

Seiko later called that outdoor focus the starting point for its sports watches, a line that led to Japan’s first diver’s watch in 1965. The Alpinist name itself went quiet in the mid-1960s and returned in 1995. The timeline picks up the story from there.

What is and isn’t documented. No Seiko source links the Alpinist to a specific mountain, range or climb. The map that follows is context, not provenance.

Seiko-documented from Seiko’s own pages   Japanese context broader history, not claimed by Seiko

  1. —
    Japanese contextAbout 70% of Japan's land area is mountainous, according to the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (GSI, MLIT). The often-quoted 73% figure is a secondary-source number.source ↗
  2. Meiji
    Japanese contextThe term 'Japanese Alps' was first used by William Gowland, an English metallurgist at the Osaka Mint (in Japan 1872-1888). Writing in A Handbook for Travellers in Central & Northern Japan (ed. Ernest Satow), he said the Hida range 'might perhaps be termed the Japanese Alps'. Walter Weston, an English missionary in Japan at intervals between 1888 and 1915, popularised the name with Mountaineering and Exploration in the Japanese Alps (1896) and is remembered as the 'father of the Japanese Alps'.source ↗
  3. 1905
    Japanese contextThe Japanese Alpine Club (Nihon Sangakukai) was founded in 1905 (Meiji 38) by Kojima Usui and others, inspired by Weston and the (British) Alpine Club; it was Japan's first mountaineering organisation. Its journal Sangaku began in 1906.source ↗
  4. 1956
    Japanese contextManaslu (8,163 m) was first climbed on 9 May 1956 by Toshio Imanishi and Gyalzen Norbu on a Japanese expedition led by Yuko (Aritsune) Maki, a landmark of postwar Japanese mountaineering.source ↗
  5. 1961?
    Seiko-documentedSeiko's German-language news page on the Prospex Alpinist relaunch says the original model was introduced in 1961 for Japanese mountain explorers and climbers, known in Japanese as 'Yama-otoko' ('mountain men'), rendered in English as 'Alpinist'.source ↗
  6. 1965
    Seiko-documentedSeiko's heritage page says its 1965 diver's watch was worn by Japanese Antarctic Research Expedition members (four times since 1966), adventurers crossing the Arctic, and mountaineers summiting Everest. This is the diver's watch, NOT the Alpinist. Seiko also says Naomi Uemura (first Japanese to summit Everest, 1970) wore a 1970 diver's watch on his 1974-76 solo dog-sled journey from Greenland to Alaska.source ↗
  7. 1981
    Seiko-documentedThe Seiko Museum opened in 1981 for Seiko's centennial and moved to Ginza in 2020 (as The Seiko Museum Ginza), marking the 160th anniversary of founder Kintaro Hattori's birth; Ginza is where he was born and started his business. The About page does not mention the Alpinist.source ↗
  8. 2016
    Japanese contextMountain Day (Yama no Hi) falls on 11 August. It was created by a 2014 amendment to the Act on National Holidays, first observed in 2016, with the legal purpose 'to gain opportunities to become familiar with mountains and to appreciate their blessings'. Mountaineering organisations formed a committee in 2010 to lobby for it.source ↗

09 / ORIENTATION方位

How the compass ring works

The Alpinist’s inner ring is not a magnetic compass. It is a rotating direction scale that you align using the sun and the hour hand. Try it: drag the sun, the hour hand or the ring, or use the sliders.

    Limitations — read before relying on it
    • Seiko describes the method as giving only a rough indication of direction. It is no substitute for a magnetic compass and map.
    • It assumes the sun moves 15° an hour and is due south (or north) at 12:00. In reality noon varies with your longitude within the time zone and the time of year, so the result can be well off.
    • Near the tropics, and around midday in summer when the sun is high, the method becomes unreliable.
    • With daylight-saving time, use 1 o’clock instead of 12 as the reference (tick the box above to see the error).
    • In the southern hemisphere the method is reversed: point 12 at the sun, and the bisector gives north.
